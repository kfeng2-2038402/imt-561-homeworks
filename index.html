I am a master’s student in MSIM, with interests in UI/UX, data visualization, and information systems.

This quarter, I want to get better at turning data into clear visual stories, especially by combining analysis, interaction design, and narrative visualization.

Documenting Iterative Process

Context of use: The Focus Window Clock is designed for students or workers who want to begin a short study session without relying on a stressful countdown timer or constantly checking their phone. The audience I am designing for is not someone who needs exact time every second, but someone who wants a calmer way to enter focus, especially in quiet indoor spaces such as a bedroom, study room, café corner, or personal workspace. This is also connected to my own experience studying in gloomy weather: when the outside environment feels gray or low-energy, time can feel slow and it becomes harder to start working.

Instead of treating time as only a number, this design turns a 25-minute focus session into a changing window scene. The goal is to support gentle time awareness: users can still see the exact remaining time when they need it, but the main experience is visual and ambient. The window gradually shifts from foggy and muted to clearer and warmer, representing the transition from mental fog to clearer focus.

Final Clock 1: Focus Window Clock

Sketch iteration documentation

Design process: My first version was closer to a traditional clock. I used a sunset window scene and imagined the sun as the main time indicator. The sun moving from left to right was intended to represent minutes, while the sun moving from top to bottom was intended to represent hours. However, after sketching and testing this idea, I realized that the mapping was not very intuitive. It was visually interesting, but it did not clearly answer why someone would use it instead of a normal clock or timer.

After receiving feedback, I reframed the design around a more specific audience and use case: students starting a short focus session. The original 2-minute version worked as a quick prototype demo, but it did not match a realistic study behavior. I changed the intended session length to 25 minutes because it better matches a Pomodoro-style study session and gives the visual transition enough time to feel meaningful. This change made the design feel less like a decorative animation and more like a useful focus tool.

Feedback-based revision: One major issue in the earlier version was accessibility. The sun path blended too much with the background, so I revised it with a dark outline and a lighter inner line. This creates stronger contrast and makes the path readable even when the sky colors are warm or bright. I also made the active sun larger, added a stronger stroke, and used size and shape in addition to color. This way, users do not have to rely only on color to understand the current progress.

I also reconsidered the meaning of the visual metaphor. The fog is not just decoration; it represents the unclear mental state at the beginning of a study session. As the timer progresses, the fog becomes lighter, the center of the window opens up, and the sky becomes warmer. This “clearing the fog” effect connects the visual change to the user’s experience of slowly entering focus. Near the end, the warmer color becomes a subtle cue that the user is close to a break.

Interaction and edge cases: I added Start, Pause, and Reset controls because a real study session is often interrupted. If the user is interrupted, they can pause instead of losing their progress. If they want to begin again, they can reset intentionally. I also removed extra status labels such as “Ready” or “In flow” because they made the interface feel crowded and less ambient. Instead, I let the window itself communicate the session stage through fog, light, color, and sun movement.

Time mapping: The sun’s movement no longer represents exact clock time. Instead, it represents progress through the focus session. The exact remaining time is still available in a high-contrast time label, but it works as a backup rather than the main experience. The main design goal is peripheral awareness: users can glance at the window and sense whether they are at the beginning, middle, or end of the session without constantly reading numbers.

I applied several information visualization principles in the final version. For mapping, the sun movement, fog clearing, and color shift all correspond to session progress. For accessibility, I increased contrast on the sun path and used size and outline to make the active sun more visible. For hierarchy, the visual scene remains primary, while the timer and buttons are placed in a separate control panel. For interaction, Start, Pause, and Reset support realistic use instead of forcing the user to restart accidentally.

Self-reflection / future work: This version is stronger because it moves beyond simply representing time and instead supports how time is experienced during a study session. It is not meant to replace every timer; it is designed for moments when students need gentle time awareness and emotional support while getting into focus. In a future version, I would add optional session lengths, such as 10, 15, or 25 minutes, so users can choose the type of focus session that fits their task. I would also test the design with students to see whether the visual transition actually helps them feel less pressure and less tempted to check their phones.

Documenting Iterative Process

This section documents the design iteration for my second final clock: the Boba Focus Clock.

Context of use: This clock is designed for studying or working in everyday spaces, such as a desk, bedroom, café corner, or study area. I often find that when I study or work for a long time, time feels slow and I can become tired or distracted. Instead of making users constantly check a digital timer, this clock embeds time into a familiar daily object: a cup of boba tea. It gives users a playful and low-pressure way to notice time passing during a focus session.

Final Clock 2: Boba Focus Clock

Sketch iteration documentation (paper/hand-drawn photos)

Design process: At first, I approached this design as a boba tea clock, using the folded angle of the straw to represent the hour and minute hands. This version kept the logic of a traditional clock while embedding time into a familiar everyday object: a cup of milk tea. However, as I continued to think about the context of use, I realized that turning it into “another kind of clock” did not create a very strong motivation for people to use it. In contrast, reframing it as a focus timer felt more meaningful and more desirable in the context of studying or working.

Because of this, I shifted the design into a focus timer for everyday work and study settings. In the final version, the liquid level represents the remaining duration of the focus session, the number of boba pearls represents the remaining minutes, and the straw no longer functions as a traditional clock hand. Instead, it becomes a subtle feedback mechanism that gently stirs the drink once every minute. This makes the passing of time visible without requiring the user to constantly read a digital number.

To make the design feel more immersive and better connected to the boba tea metaphor, I added several animation details. The boba pearls and ice cubes gently float even when the timer is not stirring, so the drink feels alive rather than static. When each minute passes, the straw slowly stirs the drink, causing the boba and ice cubes to shift slightly. The ice cubes also become smaller and gradually melt as the liquid level goes down. These changes make the design feel less like a decorative clock and more like an ambient, playful way to experience the passing of time.

I applied several information visualization principles in the final version. For hierarchy, the cup and liquid level are the main visual focus because they communicate the overall progress of the focus session. For mapping, the liquid height represents total remaining time, while the number of boba pearls represents remaining minutes. For motion, the gentle floating animation keeps the clock feeling alive without becoming too distracting, and the slower stirring animation marks each minute in a subtle way. For color, I used warm milk tea tones with pale blue ice cubes so the design feels playful, readable, and connected to the drink metaphor.

Self-reflection / future work: This version is more meaningful than my early sketch because it connects the clock to a specific use context: staying focused while studying or working. The design does not try to replace a precise digital timer, but it creates a softer way to sense time passing. In a future version, I would add an optional mode switch between a 25-minute Pomodoro session and a shorter testing/demo session. I would also consider adding a clearer completion state, such as an empty cup or a gentle message, so users can clearly feel that the focus session is finished.

HWK 5: Narrative Visualization

Why Seattle Winter Feels Longer Than Three Months

A narrative visualization about daylight, precipitation, and the weather-based feeling of Seattle winter.

Dataset: This visualization uses 2025 Seattle historical weather data from Visual Crossing. The original dataset contains daily weather records, including sunrise, sunset, precipitation, rainy days, cloud cover, temperature, and weather conditions.

Story: I chose this topic because in another class, we discussed how Seattle winter can make people feel depressed, tired, or low-energy. That conversation focused on limited daylight and frequent rain. Instead of only showing that “Seattle is rainy,” this visualization explores how shorter daylight and precipitation together can make winter feel longer than the official calendar season.

Design concept: The visualization uses a double-ring seasonal structure. The outer ring shows the weather experience across the year: color represents average daylight hours, and raindrops represent total monthly precipitation. The inner ring works as a calendar reference, showing all 12 months and highlighting official winter from December to February. A dashed blue arc marks the weather-based felt winter pattern from October to March.

Interaction: This visualization is mostly author-driven because the title, annotations, center text, and visual layout guide viewers toward one main story. It also includes a small user-driven element: viewers can hover over each month to see exact monthly values, including average daylight hours, total precipitation, rainy days, cloud cover, cloudy days, and whether the month falls within official winter.

Main takeaway: Seattle winter can feel longer than three months because darker and wetter patterns extend beyond official winter. In this visualization, “felt winter” is not an official season, but an interpretive framing based on 2025 daylight, precipitation, and cloud-cover patterns.

Data processing: I cleaned the original daily dataset into a monthly summary. I calculated daylight hours from sunrise and sunset, grouped the records by month, and summarized average daylight, total precipitation, rainy days, average cloud cover, and cloudy days. This reduced the dataset from daily-level weather records into 12 monthly rows that are easier to use in p5.js and clearer for a narrative visualization.

Encoding choices: Color is used only for average daylight hours, so darker months visually communicate shorter days. Raindrops represent total monthly precipitation, not rainy-day count. The inner ring provides calendar context, while the highlighted December–February section marks official winter. The seasonal icons in the inner ring are used only as calendar references, not as additional data encodings.

Clarity and hierarchy: I separated weather experience and calendar time into two layers. The viewer should first notice the title and center contrast between official winter and felt winter, then read the outer ring for daylight and precipitation patterns, and finally use the inner ring and hover tooltip for more detailed context.
